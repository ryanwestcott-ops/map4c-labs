Lesson: Two-Variable Data

Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson I can:

  • decide whether a situation needs one-variable or two-variable analysis
  • identify the subject, the variable(s), and the independent and dependent variables
  • choose a suitable graph, including a scatter plot for two-variable data
  • draw a scatter plot and describe its pattern (positive, negative, or no clear trend)
  • pose questions that one-variable and two-variable data can answer

How to decide: name the subject, then count how many facts are recorded about each subject. One fact → one-variable. Two facts → two-variable. Frequency is not a variable. It counts subjects.

Part 3: Describing a Scatter Plot 12 min

Strong trend: points lie close to a line. Weak trend: points are spread out but still lean one way. An outlier is a point far from the pattern of the others.

Example: Salt and Blood Pressure. Eight patients in a study. (The recommended maximum is about 6 g of salt, or 1 teaspoon, a day.)

PatientABCDEFGH
Daily salt (g)8.410.44.97.812.811.98.411.4
Systolic BP (mmHg)120164118124185163134128

a) Identify the independent and dependent variables.

c) Describe the trend.

d) Which patient fits the pattern least? Explain.

b) Draw the scatter plot.

A scatter plot shows that two variables are related. On its own it does not prove that one causes the other.
